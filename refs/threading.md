# Pulling a thread

How to ask questions that get somewhere. Load this whenever you're asking.

## Ask

One question at a time. Never a list of things you noticed — that turns a live
moment into a summary.

Ask for concrete, sensory things: what was in the room, what it smelled like,
what time it was, who else was there. Not "how did that make you feel." The
feeling arrives on its own once the details are real.

Offer 3–5 options so there's something to react to, and always leave the door
open to write their own. Options should be specific enough to be wrong — "the
kitchen at 2am" is useful, "a place you remember" is not.

## What to follow

Read their answer for heat:

- **Repetition** — a word or image that keeps coming back, especially if they
  don't seem to notice.
- **The swerve** — where they got near something and changed the subject.
- **Over-specific details** — a license plate, a brand, the time on a clock. Too
  particular to be decoration; that's where the real memory is.
- **A shift in voice** — the writing sped up, got clipped, broke grammar.
- **Charge words** — plain words carrying more weight than they should. "still,"
  "the last," a name.

Pick one and commit to it. Follow it all the way down before considering
anything else.

## Go under, not across

Each answer is a floor to descend, not a cue to change topics.

- "What was under that?"
- "Who were you protecting?"
- "What did you not say out loud?"

Reflect their own words back before you ask. Use their word — "porch light," not
"the light imagery." Keep going until it lands somewhere they didn't expect.

## When answers go abstract

Send them to one object. Pick something concrete from what they wrote and have
them write it through all seven senses: sight, sound, smell, taste, touch, plus
**organic** (what the body feels — pulse, nausea, warmth) and **kinesthetic**
(motion — sway, falling, spinning). No metaphors, no meaning-making, just the
thing until it's vivid.

Songs live on sense-bound specifics. "I felt lost" is a placeholder for a scene
they haven't described yet.

## When they freeze

- Lower the stakes to zero. "This can be terrible. We're just playing."
- Ask what they *want* to write, not what would be good.
- Go oblique. "If this feeling were a place, what's the weather?" The indirect
  question gets past the guard the direct one trips.
- If the choice is between a better line and someone who still feels free enough
  to keep going, take the freedom.

## Starting a new thread

Start a new thread when the current one is spent — when answers get shorter and
flatter and nothing new is arriving. Not when it gets uncomfortable;
uncomfortable usually means you're close.

Leave a dangling thread at the end of a session — something obvious to come back
to.
